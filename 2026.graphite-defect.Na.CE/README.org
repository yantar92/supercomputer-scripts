# A short summary about the project.
The idea is to study how introducing defects into expanded carbon with
various d-spacing affects the voltage profile.

We are looking into common point defects:
1. Mono-vacancy
2. Bi-vacancy
3. Stone-Wales

We are also looking to varying d-spacing as we did in
[[id:ArXiv-radchenko2025-rational-design-principles-ae9][Radchenko Malyi [ArXiv] (2025) Rational Design Principles for Na- and
Li-ion Carbon Anodes from Interlayer Spacing Control]]

There are also doped versions of point defects [[id:Wiley-Online-Library-tan2025-critical-role-of-05b][Xin Tan, Liangzhi Kou
Angewandte Chemie International Edition 2025]] or 1d defects like grain
boundaries, but we will ignore those.

Potentially, there is also a curvature effect, but we also ignore it
for now.

# Measurable project outcomes
1. Voltage profiles vs. defects for various d-spacing and defect density

******* Motivation

It is well-known that Na adsorption and intercalation are not
favorable in pure graphite. The various defects present in hard carbon
is what enables Na storage.  The Na storage is usually associated with
3 well-known storage mechanisms: Na adsorption on defects, Na
intercalation between graphitic layers with increased interlayer
spacing, and Na filling into pores [[id:yang2025-recent-advances-of-5bc][Yang, He Advanced Functional
Materials 2025]]. Understanding the overall Na storage thus requires
clear picture of each storage mechanism and how it is related to
the hard carbon defect structures.

Probably the most studied mechanism is Na adsorption onto defects.
This is thanks to its simplicity, where the storage is governed by
point defects that are relatively easy to study from first principles.
Numerous first-principle studies have calculated Na adsorption
energies onto various defects in graphene [[id:Wiley-Online-Library-tan2025-critical-role-of-05b][Xin Tan, Liangzhi Kou
Angewandte Chemie International Edition 2025]]; [[id:youn2021-nanometer-size-na-810][Youn, Tateyama npj
Computational Materials 2021]]; bi-layer graphene [[id:tsai2015-ab-initio-study-ea8][Tsai, Yamada Journal
of Materials Chemistry A 2015]], and, more rarely, graphite [[id:lin2025-unveiling-dominant-processes-721][Lin,
Tateyama AEM 2025]].  These studies clearly show that Na adsorption
energy (and the associated battery voltage) are strongly correlated to
the defect types.

Na intercalation is somewhat less studied. Most of the research is
drawing analogies with the previous methods suitable for Li
intercalation into graphite, often assuming similar intercalation
structures and employing similar theoretical methods (like using
cluster expansion to derive voltage vs. capacity). The common
knowledge derived from these studies is that increased d-spacing in
hard carbon is crucially important to allow Na intercalation.  The
typical graphitic d-spacing simply cannot accommodate Na and
distances close to 4.0A are required to allow Na intercalation even in
the dilute limit. More sophisticated studies reveal that storing any
significant Na concentrations require even larger d-spacing, above
4.2A [[id:tsai2015-ab-initio-study-ea8][Tsai, Yamada Journal of Materials Chemistry A 2015]], [[id:ArXiv-radchenko2025-rational-design-principles-ae9][Radchenko
Malyi [ArXiv] (2025) Rational Design Principles for Na- and Li-ion
Carbon Anodes from Interlayer Spacing Control]]. In other words,
d-spacing is considered the most important parameter enabling Na
intercalation.

Some studies explore Na intercalation in the presence of point
defects, combining the knowledge from adsorption studies and the
intercalation physics. For example, Tsai et al [[id:tsai2015-ab-initio-study-ea8][Tsai, Yamada Journal of
Materials Chemistry A 2015]] reported (using a simplified intercalation
structure of Na) that presence of single vacancy in bi-layer graphene
can enable Na intercalation corresponding to low battery voltages.
Datta et al performed a more systematic study (but on single-layer
graphene) exploring a range of defect concentrations [[id:datta2014-defective-graphene-as-cd8][Datta, Shenoy ACS
Applied Materials &amp; Interfaces 2014]]. These existing studies
clearly show that presence of defects can additionally stabilize the
intercalation compared to defect-free models that only feature the
increased d-spacing.

Indeed, the presence of point defects cannot be ignored in hard
carbon. They produce a large portion of the Na capacity (via direct Na
adsorption) and are an integral part of the microstructure, with both
experimental TEM observations and MD-based structural models
predicting defect concentrations as high as 18% [[id:wang2021-formation-and-topological-74f][Wang, Wu PCCP 2021]].
Moreover, the defect density is sensitive to synthesis conditions, so
understanding how intercalation is affected by presence of various
numbers of defects might be critical to have a more complete picture
of the Na storage in hard carbon.

We thus aim to explore a dual role of d-spacing and various
concentrations of point defects on Na intercalation in hard carbon.
In particular, we are looking to understand how both experimentally
measurable parameters (voltage profile) and low-level intercalation
chemistry are affected. 

Our idea is to add parameters in small increments, building up an
understanding of how various aspects of the hard carbon structure
affect Na storage. Unlike the more realistic models that aim to
simulate hard carbon structures that are closer to reality, we are
looking to provide a more synthetic but also controllable models,
allowing to build scientific intuition how individual structural
motifs in hard carbon affect Na storage and voltage profiles.

# Nontrivial methods used to execute the action plan.

[[id:Wiley-Online-Library-tan2025-critical-role-of-05b][Xin Tan, Liangzhi Kou Angewandte Chemie International Edition 2025]]
gives us a good example of how people approach point defects
systematically. They consider:
1. Monovacancy
2. Divacanacy (5-9-5 ring structure)
3. Stone-Wales
4. N- and O-doped monovacancies
5. edges and larges vacancies
6. All the above but on curved carbon surface

Here, we are not interested in doping (maybe later) and edges.
Let's also ignore larger vacancies for simplicity sake.

So, we should look into vacancies and Stone-Wales.  However, let's
systematically explore what is known about possible defects in hard
carbon/graphite/graphene and their concentrations (that's what point
defect studies often do not care about).

[[id:7c66b642-fe2f-4db7-a4e4-90e129667eea][Deep research: Experimental data on defect concentration in hard
carbon]] gives us an idea about the common defects - indeed, SW and
vacancies. Vacancies are tricky. If we look closer into what is known
about vacancies in graphene ([[id:57c25c32-cba0-4266-92a4-29a87f0037d9][Deep Research: Hard Carbon Vacancy
Reconstruction Mechanisms]]; [[id:kotakoski2011-stone-wales-type-206][Kotakoski, Krasheninnikov PRB 2011]]), we
will see that monovacancies are not the most stable variant - they
agglomerate into divacancies that further dissociate into 5-8-5 (as
in Xin et al) or, through high kinetic barrier, into 555-777 (the
lowest-energy vacancy configuration that is known). SW may be
considered straightforward - it is a 5-7-7-5 ring complex with net 0
curvature, however detailed studies show that introducing curvature
(up to 1.67A distortion!) can actually lower its energy, and form
"sine" and "cosine" type of distortions [[id:ma2009-stone-wales-defects-a4f][Ma, Wang PRB 2009]]. Overall, we
have:
| Defect type | Ring signature | Formation energy, eV | Note                           | Buckling         |
|-------------+----------------+----------------------+--------------------------------+------------------|
| SW (flat)   |        5-7-7-5 |            4.82-5.53 | Depends on boundary conditions | none             |
| SW (curved) |        5-7-7-5 |            4.59-5.26 | same                           | up to 1.67A      |
| MV          |            5-9 |              7.3-7.8 | Magnetic!                      | local protrusion |
| DV          |          5-8-5 |              7.2-8.0 |                                | none             |
| DV          |        555-777 |              6.5-7.1 | high kinetic barrier           | yes              |
| DV          |    5555-6-7777 |              6.7-7.3 |                                |                  |

Curved SW is interesting. While I do not expect it to appear for small
d-spacing because of geometric constraints, it may be something to
consider for larger d-spacing. We may need to be mindful and use
perturbations for every d-spacing rather than simply scaling up the
relaxed systems and assume that the optimal geometry will be
preserved.

MV has been reported to be magnetic. This is important. I further
checked what people report about Na adsorption on MV and found [[id:lin2025-unveiling-dominant-processes-721][Lin,
Tateyama AEM 2025]] (Table S8) where they show -0.95eV (no ISPIN) vs
-1.13eV (ISPIN) Na adsorption on MV. So, energetics is affected.
MV is somewhat different from other graphite defects as they do not
create dangling bonds, so we may not need magnetic calculations for DV
and SW, but something to double-check for sure.

[[id:lin2025-unveiling-dominant-processes-721][Lin, Tateyama AEM 2025]] also reported interesting observations about
two MV located on top of each other in expanded graphite (they explore
slit pore with d-spacings of 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0nm). They show that the
second MV on top can induce a "bridge" of Na connecting the two MV
defects. This means that MV-MV interaction may be extremely important.
Indeed, it appears to be a common knowledge that defects in graphite
are highly sensitive to self-interaction. [[id:ma2009-stone-wales-defects-a4f][Ma, Wang PRB 2009]] report as
much as 0.5eV difference in SW formation energies depending on
supercell choice. And it is not even only about supercell size, but
even about the shape (square vs. hexagonal lattice vectors). So,
relative positions of the defects may affect our results - both
defects directly placed into the supercell and periodic images.

To sum up, key factors to watch out:
1. Everything should be perturbed, including out of plane.
   Maybe even try in-phase/anti-phase perturbations (that's something
   SW curvature is affected by)
2. MV is magnetic. Others - need to double-check
3. We need to do supercell studies.
   Not just by size, but also by different aspect ratios.


The supercell size dependence means that our reference "defected
expanded graphite" energy will be highly sensitive to supercell size,
which is a proxy for defect concentration. This is good - we can
reasonably anticipate that Na intercalation will be strongly affected
by the defect concentration (consistent with out observation in
[[id:ArXiv-radchenko2025-rational-design-principles-ae9][Radchenko, Malyi ArXiv 2025]] that changes in carbon energy caused by
d-spacing are one of the key factors stabilizing Na intercalation).
But we also need to be careful using reasonable defect concentrations
supported by literature - concentration range should correspond to
real hard carbon, if possible.

Unfortunately, I did not find good experimental data on the defect
concentrations in hard carbon. The defects are clearly present,
clearly abundant, clearly depend on the annealing temperature (and
never fully "annealed away"), but no numbers. LLM ([[id:7c66b642-fe2f-4db7-a4e4-90e129667eea][Deep research:
Experimental data on defect concentration in hard carbon]]) estimates
between ~1%-4% of non-hexagonal rings judging from some TEM reports,
but we can only get estimates like "X rings per nm of surface" from
there - very rough estimate that is also not necessarily reliable due
to the local nature of TEM probe.

There is reverse diffraction fit presented in [[id:jurkiewicz2017-modelling-of-glass-f61][Jurkiewicz, Burian JAC
2017]], but I am a bit skeptical about its results because they
essentially use the same technique as in [[id:stratford2021-correlating-local-structure-14e][Stratford, Grey JACS 2021]]
obtaining wildly different results. Anyway, Jurkiewicz gives some data
on the fitted defect concentrations for hard carbons annealed at
different temperatures, although they themselves put a big caveat
behind exact defect types they have in the fit:

From the above table, we can associate 9-rings with MV. I would not
trust Tersoff/RBO-II/EDIP too much -- they give fairly small hexagon
counts. Other data indicates very small concentrations, 0-0.8%. We can
stretch it up to 3-4% if we trust reverse diffraction fit above for
GC800 (low-temerature annealing), but I would expect [[id:wang2021-formation-and-topological-74f][Wang, Wu PCCP
2021]] to plot it if 9-rings were observed in significant amounts at the
early stages of the annealing, so the real upper bound is probably
lower.

8-rings are a signature of DV (5-8-5 variant); the range is -
0.3-3.4%. Again, upper bound is a bit of a stretch here Maybe 1.8-2%
is a more reasonable upper limit.

SW and dissociated DV should contribute to 5- and 7-rings, creating
equal amount of each. If all the defects in HC can be represented by
MV, DV, and SW, we should expect that removing 5-8-5-bound and
5-9-bound 5-rings from the total should leave us with equal amount of
5- and 7-rings. This is clearly not the case, either because other,
more exotic defects are present (e.g. complex sp3 3d-bonding, carbon
edges, etc), or because some of the defects create complex clustering
like 555-777 DV sharing one (or more) of its rings with 5-9 MV or
5-8-5 DV. For simplicity, let's estimate the SW concentration range
from 5-rings and 7-rings numbers as bounds (remember that 5-7-7-5
consumes 2 rings each): 1.1-4.6% with 4.6% likely being an
overestimation and more real range -- 1.1-2.0%. Note how we never get
0% SW though - this may be important factor to consider, especially
given potential strong defect-defect interactions. We likely need to
build at least one test model that includes SW + some other
defect. That should either be two isolated defects (e.g. SW + DV) or
two defects sharing a ring. I am inclined to focus on the latter as it
feels like something with lower energy -- will need to check defect
reaction. Rough estimate of concentrations is "delta 5/7a": 0-1.2%.

Takeaway about concentrations:
| Defect type         | Min concentration | Max concentration | More realistic max |
|---------------------+-------------------+-------------------+--------------------|
| MV (5-9)            |                0% |                3% |               0.8% |
| DV (5-8-5)          |              0.3% |              3.4% |               1.8% |
| SW (5-7-7-5)        |              1.1% |              4.6% |               2.0% |
| SW + MV (tentative) |                0% |              5.6% |               1.2% |

The concentrations are very small. For realistic max MV concentration
we need a supercell of at least 125 atoms. If we plan to use ATAT (or
anything else that will make use of cluster expansion with full
enumeration), enumerating large cell is practically limited around
50-60 atoms, according to previous runs. So, realistic supercell we
can manage is probably 4x4x2 (for AA graphite) and 4x4x1 (for AB
graphite). That is 64 atoms, limiting our concentrations at 1.56%
lower bound. If we go up to 6x6x2 (0.69%) even simple DFT relaxation
becomes extremely slow, taking more than 12 hours for complex systems
(with perturbation). So, something closer 1% (5x5x2) is probably the
practical hard limit for DFT. If we want to look into smaller defect
concentrations, using MACE will be a must.

In any case, even if we do need to use MACE to full results, we need
to have /some/ DFT runs at least to get the training data. We also
need to see if ATAT can practically handle our system. So, we will
first need to get a couple of trial calculations, starting from some
fixed defect concentration, and testing all the steps to make sure
that we know all the possible caveats that may show up.

As the first cycle, let's choose 2 defects with constant density +
fixed d-spacing and do all the anticipated calculation steps, aiming
to produce voltage profile:
1. SW defect with 1/64 concentration, d-spacing = 4.58Å
2. MV defect with the same 1/64 concentration, d-spacing = 4.58Å

 *Step 1*: Look into defect energy and geometry

The aim here is getting a fully relaxed defect and configure DFT
parameters we will be using later. Our starting point will be previous
calculations from [[id:ArXiv-radchenko2025-rational-design-principles-ae9][Radchenko Malyi ArXiv 2025]] - we will reuse
ENCUT=500eV, functional (=optB88-vdW=), and other parameters as the
reference
(=2025.graphite-exanded.Na.CE/08.CE.Me_pos_relax.KPOINTS.10k.optB88.ENCUT.550.scale.c.0.00/AA/d.4.58/0/ATAT=). We
also start from AA stacking, because Na is expected to intercalate
there easier and the ATAT fits may be faster.

For 1/64 defect density (either SW or MV), we will need to create a
supercell, and add a *single* defect inside. Consider multiple
supercells: 4x4x2, 2x8x2, 4x2x4, 5x1x4 - formation energies may depend
on the supercell shape, as reported in other studies. We also need to
consider ISPIN 1 vs. 2 for magnetic vs. non-magnetic, especially for
MV.
: imdg derive <reference_system> supercell 4x4x2
: <then also need to add a defect into the supercell>

We cannot use the same relaxation constrains as in previous study -
previously we could freeze carbons in place, but with the defect, we
must allow carbons to move along all 3 dimensions, to find the optimal
geometry. So, need to make sure that VASP inputs will not have any
selective dynamics constrains. We thus need to fix the lattice
vectors (ISIF=2), making sure that the AA stacking and d-spacing (at
least, on average) do not change. *We still need to watch for
stacking - AA may be unstable in the absence of Na. If AA is indeed
unstable, may need to fix a single carbon in each layer to avoid
AA->AB shifts.*

It will be important to do the perturbation before relaxation of the
pure defect. In my tests, 0.4Å was too much, 0.2Å is probably an
optimal value:
: imdg derive <supercell_with_vacancy_folder> perturb --distance 0.2

Relaxed geometries are expected to form ring complexes as described in
the literature. Note that what people call "5-rings" may not be
displayed by VESTA - we may need to increase the bond length cutoff to
see such rings. The exact cutoff will depend on the defect
concentration with more isolated defects relaxing to smaller bond
lengths. See [[id:8396c23b-dc1c-4218-97ce-68006379387f][[2025-12-17 Wed] vacancy, bi-vacancy perturbation,
relaxation, Weekly report; 5-ring size correlates with supercell]].

We also need to check and compare energies for different supercell
shapes. If they are the same - good. If not, will need to discuss;
probably we should better choose the same (and single) final supercell
for next steps, maybe with the lowest energy.

 *Step 2: Build Na sublattice and check adsorption energies*

Once we have the supercell + defect geometry, we can put single Na
inside and see where it goes.
: imdg derive <supercell geometry output> ins --step=0.1 --multithread Na

VASP parameters should mostly be good to go, so we just need to run
things and see where Na goes. However, we will need to double-check
the final unique sites for magnetic vs. non-magnetic (ISPIN) - Na
adsorption on defect can be magnetic [[id:lin2025-unveiling-dominant-processes-721][Lin, Tateyama AEM 2025]]. If
magnetism is important, will need to switch it on for all the
subsequent runs.

We expect that Na will have stable sites on top of the defect site -
the energy should be low. The energy should also be consistent with
literature. We can look into [[id:Wiley-Online-Library-tan2025-critical-role-of-05b][Lin Kou Angewandte Chemie International
Edition 2025]] (maybe also find more papers) for comparison. The values
will likely not be the same - most of the literature data is for
graphene or for different d-spacing than hours. Additionally compare
with [[id:youn2021-nanometer-size-na-810][Youn, Tateyama npj Computational Materials 2021]] (Fig. 5) and [[id:lin2025-unveiling-dominant-processes-721][Lin,
Tateyama AEM 2025]] (supplementary) - they have expanded graphite data,
although not always directly comparable.

Also, literature consistently reports very specific cluster shapes on
top of defects (see [[id:youn2021-nanometer-size-na-810][Youn, Tateyama npj Computational Materials 2021]]
and [[id:lin2025-unveiling-dominant-processes-721][Lin, Tateyama AEM 2025]]), so it is a good idea to run Na insertion
scan into graphite + defect + Na sitting on top of the defect
system. I expect this to reveal additional sublattice sites in the
vicinity of the adsorbed Na. If this turns out to be the case, we will
need to discuss and maybe consider alternative way to build
sublattice, iterating full Na insertion scan not just for single Na
insertion, but also for multiple Na.

 *Step 3: Fit the cluster expansion and compute voltage profile*

Once we have possible Na sites, we can build a sublattice. ~imdg fill~
command should auto-build the sublattice POSCAR with all possible Na
sites occupied. Depending on the actual results, the sublattice may or
may not be realistic - we may get Na sites close to each other. If we
get non-trivial sublattice, will need to discuss and decide how to
proceed. Tentatively, will remove some sites and see whether the fit
can work.

With POSCAR for sublattice, we can generate =lat.in= file for ATAT
using pymatgen (=pmg convert= command can convert into ATAT
format). That file can then be lightly cleaned up - we will need to
mark Na sites as Vac, Na identifying the concentration scan.
Then, with VASP input + lat.in file in place, ~gorun-maps~ command can
automatically setup ATAT run. We will use 10000 kpoint density (same
as in other calculations, but must be passed as parameter to
~gorun-maps~ when processing ATAT-generated structures).

We are not yet sure whether ATAT itself will work well for
supercell. ATAT will certainly try to reduce the supercell into
primitive lattice. However, with the defect, primitive lattice will
likely be the supercell itself, so ATAT may actually work fine. But
still need to watch for what kinds of structures ATAT will
generate. If we start getting weird supercells, may need to switch to
alternative structure generator. Most likely, it will be pymatgen's
generator that can fully enumerate lattice. Will discuss if need to go
this way. A preliminary expect that ATAT will actually run just fine
with defect.

Additionally, given that we are changing the relaxation constraints,
we will need to repeat our calculations from [[id:ArXiv-radchenko2025-rational-design-principles-ae9][Radchenko Malyi ArXiv
2025]] with defect-free expanded graphite. Here, we will definitely need
pymatgen's generator to make sure that we directly compare for the
same supercell. (In theory, it should not matter, however; but good
idea to be careful).

If everything works, well we should be able to fit the results and get
voltage plots once the fit converges (=imdg visualize atat= command
will show the current progress of the fit).

 *Step 4: Repeat steps 1-3 for different graphite stacking, d-spacing,
 defect concentrations, and maybe add more defect types*

If everything works well, we can scale the calculations to other
d-spacings, defect types, and defect concentrations.  We will likely
be limited to increasing defect concentrations. For lower
concentrations, we may need to consider MACE - will need to try and
see.

It may also be tempting to scale up the Na sublattices when changing
d-spacing, but it might be a mistake - will need to re-check and see
how Na positions are affected when the defect is getting further away
from neighboring carbon layer. Also, defect geometries might change
with d-spacing. So, should follow the steps carefully.

I am investigating pore filling mechanism during Na sodiation into
hard carbon. It is generally believed that pore filling is associated
with low, plateau voltage regime. Some papers claim that the
underlying mechanistic picture is that Na nucleates and "wets" the
pore edges, eventually overcoming nucleation barrier and not requiring
energy to grow further. What appears to be missing though is the
connection to the kinetics. It is also known for hard carbon + Na that
low-voltage regime is associated with sloppy diffusion, but the origin
of the slow diffusion is not clear, especially considering stable pore
filling suggested by DFT calculations. I hypothesize that the key to
this picture is nucleation barrier, which may depend on the pore size
and needs to be overcame to induce stable pore filling - this barrier
is higher than nominal voltage and is overcame stochastically, with
pores randomly overcoming the barrier and beeing filled -- the process
that requires time and is connected to standard nucleation theory
frequencies. If my hypothesis is correct, we should experimentally see
some correlation between diffusion coefficients and pore sizes (as
pore size presumably controls the nucleation barrier with sub-0.5nm
pores not allowing nucleation, ~1nm pores producing easy storage and
large pores increasing the diffusion barriers). Please review the
attached papers and ALSO LOOK INTO OTHER PUBLICATIONS. Analysize the
possible contradictions with my ideas and highlight if similar ideas
(kinetics & nucleation barriers) have been expressed.
